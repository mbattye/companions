ΜίεζαMieza, 343 BC

A philosopher, a thirteen-year-old prince, and his friends, in a grove of springs and caves.

Philip of Macedon sent for Aristotle and gave him the sanctuary of the Nymphs at Mieza as a schoolroom. For around three years the prince Alexander and a cohort of young nobles studied ethics and politics, Homer and medicine: how to live and how to govern. Centuries later Plutarch reported that visitors were still shown Aristotle’s stone seats and shady walks.

The friends who grew up beside Alexander became his Hetairoi, the Companions. What they received was not a syllabus. It was a way of seeing the world as one connected thing, and a habit of asking why.

We think every serious student deserves the same. For the first time in history it is possible: a tutor with patience for any question, available to anyone. What the machine cannot supply is the rest of it. You still need the discipline, the companions, the workshop and the nerve. That is what this school is for.

ΝόμοιSeven convictions

What we believe

θαῦμα

Wonder comes first.

“It is owing to their wonder that men both now and at first began to philosophise” (Aristotle, Metaphysics I.2). Curiosity is the engine, and the curriculum exists to feed it.

κόσμος

Everything is connected.

Geometry lives in architecture, statistics in medicine, Homer in military history. Learn the whole map, not one corner of it.

τέχνη

Hands and head together.

The workshop is a classroom. You don’t understand a thing until you can make it, break it and mend it.

ἀλήθεια

Argue with your teachers.

Aristotle disagreed with Plato, his teacher for twenty years, and said we must honour truth above our friends (Nicomachean Ethics I.6). Here, everyone may be questioned.

ἑταῖροι

Learn among companions.

Learn in a cohort, not a crowd. Peers who walk, argue, compete and build beside you teach as much as any master.

μηχανή

The machine is a tutor, not a substitute.

AI can give every student an Aristotle. It must never do the thinking, the writing or the making for them.

σχολή

Leisure is the point.

The Greek word for leisure, scholē, gave us “school”. Learning isn’t just preparation for life. It is one of the best parts of living.

IHow the Companions learnt

Structured, or open-ended? Both, deliberately.

The sources are thin. Plutarch wrote four centuries later, and much of what we “know” is reconstruction. But the outline is clear, and it shows a school in between: a hard, structured frame around open, curious inquiry.

The frame was Macedonian. Philip had formalised the royal pages: noble boys raised at court who guarded the king, rode, hunted and served at table. The boys at Mieza were of that age and class, so their frame was probably the same: discipline, the body, duty and rank. Their days were not their own.

The inquiry was Aristotle’s. His method, on the evidence of his own works, was dialectic: start from what people already believe (endoxa), set out the puzzles (aporiai) and reason towards what survives. Plutarch says Alexander received his ethical and political teaching, and also the deeper “acroamatic” teachings kept for pupils. Aristotle passed on a love of medicine: Alexander later prescribed treatments for his friends. Alexander also carried a text of the Iliad corrected by Aristotle. He kept it under his pillow beside a dagger, and later stored it in a precious casket taken from Darius.

The Greek ideal set the balance. In Politics VIII Aristotle names the four customary subjects: letters, gymnastics, music and sometimes drawing. He argues that education should aim at noble leisure, not only usefulness. He warns against making the young “banausic”, trained for a trade and nothing else. In Book VII he advises no lessons before five, only play, movement and stories.

The balance we take from this: a fixed rhythm and set texts, with freedom of question inside them. Open curiosity fails without structure, and structure deadens without curiosity.

IIThe inheritances

What we take from each room

Greece gives us the aims. Later workshops showed how to turn learning into making, and making into change.

335 BC · Athens

The Lyceum

Aristotle’s own school. By tradition, rigorous lectures in the morning for pupils and open ones in the evening for the public, with discussion in the covered walk (peripatos).

  • Research as collective work: collect everything, then theorise
  • Two registers: rigorous and public
  • A library and a museum of specimens
c. 1470 · Florence

The bottega

In Verrocchio’s workshop Leonardo started with menial jobs, then did drawing, casting and painting, and finished parts of the master’s pictures.

  • Apprenticeship: graduated responsibility on real commissions
  • Drawing daily as a way of thinking
  • The notebook: questions, sketches, lists
c. 1765 · Birmingham

The Lunar Society

Boulton, Watt, Wedgwood, Priestley and Erasmus Darwin met monthly near the full moon, so they had light to ride home: on Sundays at first, then on Mondays after Priestley, a minister, arrived in 1780.

  • Makers and theorists at the same table
  • Demonstration over declamation
  • Friendship as infrastructure for invention
2000s · Cupertino

Apple

Small teams, fierce editing and one guiding line: technology is not enough on its own, and it has to be married to the liberal arts.

  • Taste is trained: study the best, reject the rest
  • Focus means saying no to a thousand things
  • Integration: the whole product, not the part
2010s–20s · Hawthorne

SpaceX

Reasoning from first principles, hardware-rich testing and a willingness to explode a prototype to learn faster.

  • Question the requirement before the design
  • Build, test, fail, fix, all in weeks
  • One named engineer owns each part end to end
Now

The machine

In 1984 Benjamin Bloom found that one-to-one tutored students outperformed classroom peers by about two standard deviations. Later studies find smaller gains, but tutoring remains among the most effective teaching known. At that scale it used to be unaffordable. It no longer is.

  • A Socratic tutor for every student
  • A sparring partner that argues the other side
  • Never a ghost-writer (see the rules below)
IIIMethods of imparting knowledge

How it is taught

ἈκρόασιςLecture
The morning lecture.

One hard idea from someone who has mastered it. Short and dense, with questions allowed. Aristotle’s surviving works are largely lecture notes of this kind.

ΠερίπατοςThe walk
Dialectic in motion.

Pairs walk and argue the morning’s idea. Walking loosens thinking, and the name of Aristotle’s school may come from its covered walk.

ἜλεγχοςExamination
Socratic questioning.

A tutor, human or machine, draws out a belief and then tests it until it breaks or holds.

ἈπορίαιPuzzles
Aristotle’s method.

Collect what the wise and the many already say, list the puzzles honestly, then work towards a resolution. This works for any subject.

ἘργαστήριονWorkshop
Apprenticeship.

Real work for real users, with responsibility that grows. The bottega model applied to code, machines, buildings and ventures.

ὙπομνήματαNotebooks
The commonplace book.

A hand-kept notebook of quotations, sketches, questions and failures, like Leonardo’s notebooks and Marcus Aurelius’ notes to himself. Memory is built, not searched.

ΣυμπόσιονSymposium
Weekly conversation.

Dinner, one question and a rule that everyone speaks. Cross-disciplinary by design, as at the Lunar Society.

ἘπίδειξιςDemonstration
Ship it in public.

At the end of every term, show what you made to people who owe you nothing. Keynote, launch or exhibition: the work must speak.

ΔιδασκαλίαTeaching
Teach to learn.

Seneca: homines dum docent discunt, people learn while they teach (Letters 7.8). Older students teach younger ones at every stage.

ΠεῖραTrial
The iteration loop.

Hypothesise, build, test, fail, fix. It applies to an essay draft, an experiment or a rocket engine alike.

IVLatitude to criticise

Everyone may be questioned, including the teacher.

“While both are dear, piety requires us to honour truth above our friends.”Aristotle, on disagreeing with Plato, Nicomachean Ethics I.6

  1. Nothing is exempt.Not the teacher, the textbook, the canon or this curriculum. A school that can’t be criticised has stopped learning.
  2. Earn the right first.Before you attack a view, state it so well its author would say, “I wish I’d put it that way.”
  3. Attack the argument, never the person.Companions disagree fiercely and stay companions.
  4. Bring a better proposal or a test.Criticism that only tears down is cheap. Offer an alternative or an experiment that would settle it.
  5. Argue in the symposium, commit in the workshop.Debate freely before a decision. Once the build starts, build, and bring the evidence back to the next symposium.
VThe order

Where do we begin: philosophy, history or science?

Aristotle answers it himself. In the Physics he says we must proceed from what is more knowable to us to what is more knowable by nature. In the Posterior Analytics he explains that what is nearer the senses is more knowable to us. That means starting with particulars and the senses and working towards principles. So we begin with the concrete world: stories, specimens, drawing, music, making things with our hands.

History is the first story, told as a narrative running from the Big Bang to breakfast. We return to it in Stage Ε as a question of causes: why states rise, why wars start, why ideas take hold.

Science follows the instruments. Once a student has language, number, logic and code, nature can be measured and not only admired.

Philosophy comes twice. Its tools (logic and dialectic) arrive early. Philosophy as a subject (questions about being, knowledge and mind) waits until a student has enough of the world to reason about.

Ethics and politics come last, on purpose. Aristotle says the young are not proper students of political science because they lack experience of life. Elsewhere he notes that the young can become good mathematicians but not practically wise, because practical wisdom (phronēsis) needs experience of particulars. That is why Stage Ϛ is built around real responsibility.

Every domain appears at every stage at a different depth, as a spiral and not a ladder. The stages use Greek numerals, including ϛ for six. Ages are a guide for a young student; an adult can move through the early stages in months.

VIITime

How the hours are spent

The balance shifts as a student grows. Play-heavy at first, then instruments, then workshop, then responsibility. Study never falls below a fifth of the week, and making never below 15 per cent.

VIIIThe machine

Rules for the tutor

An AI tutor is the most powerful teaching instrument ever built, and the easiest to misuse. These rules keep it an Aristotle rather than a ghost-writer.

  1. Attempt first.The student tries before the machine speaks. A first attempt that fails still does most of the learning.
  2. The tutor asks more than it tells.Its default mode is Socratic. It gives answers only after the student has reasoned.
  3. Never outsource the making.It may critique the essay, the proof or the code. It may not write them.
  4. Verify everything.Machines can be confidently wrong. Trace every claim to a primary source, a measurement or a proof.
  5. Use it as an opponent.Ask it to argue the other side at full strength, then answer it.
  6. Explain it back.If you can’t teach it to the machine, and then to a younger companion, you don’t know it.
  7. Keep the notebook by hand.The machine remembers for you. The notebook makes you remember.
IXThe library

A reading list for every domain

Each list has three tiers. Begin is an accessible way in. Classic is the primary texts and the modern classics of each field, in the original or a good translation. Deeper is modern work for when you want the full picture. Read the primary texts; let the tutor help you through them, not around them.

Begin with wonder. End with something made.
