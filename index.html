    ΜίεζαMieza, 343 BC

    A philosopher, a thirteen-year-old prince, and his friends, in a grove of springs and caves.

    Philip of Macedon sent for Aristotle and gave him the sanctuary of the Nymphs at Mieza as a schoolroom. For around three years the prince Alexander and a cohort of young nobles studied ethics and politics, Homer and medicine: how to live and how to govern. Centuries later Plutarch reported that visitors were still shown Aristotle’s stone seats and shady walks.

    The friends who grew up beside Alexander became his Hetairoi, the Companions. What they received was not a syllabus. It was a way of seeing the world as one connected thing, and a habit of asking why.

    We think every serious student deserves the same. For the first time in history it is possible: a tutor with patience for any question, available to anyone. What the machine cannot supply is the rest of it. You still need the discipline, the companions, the workshop and the nerve. That is what this school is for.

    ΝόμοιSeven convictions

    What we believe

    θαῦμα

    Wonder comes first.

    “It is owing to their wonder that men both now and at first began to philosophise” (Aristotle, Metaphysics I.2). Curiosity is the engine, and the curriculum exists to feed it.

    κόσμος

    Everything is connected.

    Geometry lives in architecture, statistics in medicine, Homer in military history. Learn the whole map, not one corner of it.

    τέχνη

    Hands and head together.

    The workshop is a classroom. You don’t understand a thing until you can make it, break it and mend it.

    ἀλήθεια

    Argue with your teachers.

    Aristotle disagreed with Plato, his teacher for twenty years, and said we must honour truth above our friends (Nicomachean Ethics I.6). Here, everyone may be questioned.

    ἑταῖροι

    Learn among companions.

    Learn in a cohort, not a crowd. Peers who walk, argue, compete and build beside you teach as much as any master.

    μηχανή

    The machine is a tutor, not a substitute.

    AI can give every student an Aristotle. It must never do the thinking, the writing or the making for them.

    σχολή

    Leisure is the point.

    The Greek word for leisure, scholē, gave us “school”. Learning prepares you for life. It is also one of the best parts of it.

    How they learntThe inheritances
    IHow the Companions learnt

    Deliberately structured and open-ended.

    The sources are thin. Plutarch wrote four centuries later, and much of what we “know” is reconstruction. But the outline is clear, and it shows a school in between: a hard, structured frame around open, curious inquiry.

    The frame was Macedonian. Philip had formalised the royal pages: noble boys raised at court who guarded the king, rode, hunted and served at table. The boys at Mieza were of that age and class, so their frame was probably the same: discipline, the body, duty and rank. Their days were not their own.

    The inquiry was Aristotle’s. His method, on the evidence of his own works, was dialectic: start from what people already believe (endoxa), set out the puzzles (aporiai) and reason towards what survives. Plutarch says Alexander received his ethical and political teaching, and also the deeper “acroamatic” teachings kept for pupils. Aristotle passed on a love of medicine: Alexander later prescribed treatments for his friends. Alexander also carried a text of the Iliad corrected by Aristotle. He kept it under his pillow beside a dagger, and later stored it in a precious casket taken from Darius.

    The Greek ideal set the balance. In Politics VIII Aristotle names the four customary subjects: letters, gymnastics, music and sometimes drawing. He argues that education should aim at noble leisure, not only usefulness. He warns against making the young “banausic”, trained for a trade and nothing else. In Book VII he advises no lessons before five, only play, movement and stories.

    The balance we take from this: a fixed rhythm and set texts, with freedom of question inside them. Open curiosity fails without structure, and structure deadens without curiosity.

    IIThe inheritances

    What we take from each room

    Greece gives us the aims. Later rooms kept learning alive, gave it institutions and methods, and turned it into making and change.

    335 BC · Athens

    The Lyceum

    Aristotle’s own school. By tradition, rigorous lectures in the morning for pupils and open ones in the evening for the public, with discussion in the covered walk (peripatos).

    • Research as collective work: collect everything, then theorise
    • Two registers: rigorous and public
    • A library, a shrine to the Muses and maps of the known world
    c. 280 BC · Alexandria

    The Mouseion

    Founded under the first Ptolemies, probably on the Lyceum’s model. Scholars were paid to study, and ate at a common table. Eratosthenes ran the Library; Herophilus pioneered anatomy; Euclid taught in the city.

    • Give scholars time: it is the scarcest resource
    • Collect every text, then correct it
    • Measure the world: Eratosthenes and the size of the Earth
    c. 680 · Wearmouth–Jarrow

    Bede’s monastery

    Benedict Biscop brought books from Rome to twin monasteries in Northumbria. Bede entered at seven, stayed for life, and wrote that he had always delighted “in learning, teaching or writing”.

    • The library as lifeline: copy, keep, teach
    • A rule for the day: work, prayer and reading at fixed hours
    • Scholarship that serves: Bede’s reckoning of time
    c. 830 · Baghdad

    The House of Wisdom

    Under the Abbasid caliphs, translators such as Hunayn ibn Ishaq turned Greek medicine, mathematics and Aristotle into Arabic, and scholars such as al-Khwarizmi built on them. How formal an institution it was is still debated.

    • To translate a work, you must understand it
    • Inherit, then extend: al-Khwarizmi’s algebra
    • Many languages, one library
    c. 1200 · Bologna, Paris, Oxford

    The university

    Masters and students formed guilds (universitates) with their own rules and degrees. Teaching meant the lecture on a set text and the formal disputation. From 1255 the Paris arts course set nearly all of Aristotle.

    • Scholars set their own standards
    • Disputation: defend a thesis against all comers
    • The degree as a licence to teach
    c. 1470 · Florence

    The bottega

    In Verrocchio’s workshop Leonardo started with menial jobs, then did drawing, casting and painting, and finished parts of the master’s pictures.

    • Apprenticeship: graduated responsibility on real commissions
    • Drawing daily as a way of thinking
    • The notebook: questions, sketches, lists
    1603 · Rome

    The Lincei

    Federico Cesi, aged eighteen, founded the academy with three friends and named it for the sharp-eyed lynx. Galileo joined in 1611, and the Lincei published his Letters on Sunspots and The Assayer.

    • See for yourself: the lynx’s eye
    • Friends bound by a common purpose
    • A shared name: members signed their work “Linceo”
    1660 · London

    The Royal Society

    Founded after a lecture at Gresham College. Robert Hooke, its Curator of Experiments, showed new trials at meetings. Its motto, Nullius in verba, means take nobody’s word for it. Newton was its President from 1703.

    • Evidence over authority
    • Show the experiment before witnesses
    • Publish it: Philosophical Transactions, from 1665
    c. 1765 · Birmingham

    The Lunar Society

    Boulton, Watt, Wedgwood, Priestley and Erasmus Darwin met monthly near the full moon, so they had light to ride home: on Sundays at first, then on Mondays after Priestley, a minister, arrived in 1780.

    • Makers and theorists at the same table
    • Demonstration over declamation
    • Friendship as infrastructure for invention
    1921 · Copenhagen

    Bohr’s institute

    Niels Bohr’s institute drew young physicists from across Europe, Heisenberg, Pauli and Dirac among them. They argued in seminars, on walks and over table tennis, and the Copenhagen interpretation of quantum mechanics took shape.

    • Gather the best young minds and let them argue
    • Informality: seminars, walks, games
    • A master who thinks aloud with his students
    1970 · Palo Alto

    Xerox PARC

    A research centre that built the Alto (1973), with its windowed screen and mouse, networked it by Ethernet and used it every day. Alan Kay’s Learning Research Group designed computers for children to learn with. After Steve Jobs visited in 1979, its ideas reached the Macintosh.

    • Build the tools you will use, then use them daily
    • Children as the test of a good tool
    • Hire well, then give people room to work
    2010s–20s · Hawthorne

    SpaceX

    Reasoning from first principles, hardware-rich testing and a willingness to explode a prototype to learn faster.

    • Question the requirement before the design
    • Build, test, fail, fix, all in weeks
    • One named engineer owns each part end to end
    Now

    The machine

    In 1984 Benjamin Bloom found that one-to-one tutored students outperformed classroom peers by about two standard deviations. Later studies find smaller gains, but tutoring remains among the most effective teaching known. At that scale it used to be unaffordable. It no longer is.

    • A Socratic tutor for every student
    • A sparring partner that argues the other side
    • Never a ghost-writer (see the rules below)

    That is the inheritance. Now the order of study.

    IMethods of imparting knowledge

    How it is taught

    ἈκρόασιςLecture
    The morning lecture.

    One hard idea from someone who has mastered it. Short and dense, with questions allowed. Aristotle’s surviving works are largely lecture notes of this kind.

    ΠερίπατοςThe walk
    Dialectic in motion.

    Pairs walk and argue the morning’s idea. Walking loosens thinking, and the name of Aristotle’s school may come from its covered walk.

    ἜλεγχοςExamination
    Socratic questioning.

    A tutor, human or machine, draws out a belief and then tests it until it breaks or holds.

    ἈπορίαιPuzzles
    Aristotle’s method.

    Collect what the wise and the many already say, list the puzzles honestly, then work towards a resolution. This works for any subject.

    ἘργαστήριονWorkshop
    Apprenticeship.

    Real work for real users, with responsibility that grows. The bottega model applied to code, machines, buildings and ventures.

    ὙπομνήματαNotebooks
    The commonplace book.

    A hand-kept notebook of quotations, sketches, questions and failures, like Leonardo’s notebooks and Marcus Aurelius’ notes to himself. Memory is built, not searched.

    ΣυμπόσιονSymposium
    Weekly conversation.

    Dinner, one question and a rule that everyone speaks. Cross-disciplinary by design, as at the Lunar Society.

    ἘπίδειξιςDemonstration
    Ship it in public.

    At the end of every term, show what you made to people who owe you nothing. Keynote, launch or exhibition: the work must speak.

    ΔιδασκαλίαTeaching
    Teach to learn.

    Seneca: homines dum docent discunt, people learn while they teach (Letters 7.8). Older students teach younger ones at every stage.

    ΠεῖραTrial
    The iteration loop.

    Hypothesise, build, test, fail, fix. It applies to an essay draft, an experiment or a rocket engine alike.

    IILatitude to criticise

    Everyone may be questioned, including the teacher.

    “While both are dear, piety requires us to honour truth above our friends.”Aristotle, on disagreeing with Plato, Nicomachean Ethics I.6

    1. Nothing is exempt.Not the teacher, the textbook, the canon or this curriculum. A school that can’t be criticised has stopped learning.
    2. Earn the right first.Before you attack a view, state it so well its author would say, “I wish I’d put it that way.”
    3. Attack the argument, never the person.Companions disagree fiercely and stay companions.
    4. Bring a better proposal or a test.Criticism that only tears down is cheap. Offer an alternative or an experiment that would settle it.
    5. Argue in the symposium, commit in the workshop.Debate freely before a decision. Once the build starts, build, and bring the evidence back to the next symposium.
    IIIThe order

    Where we begin: philosophy, history and science.

    Aristotle answers it himself. In the Physics he says we must proceed from what is more knowable to us to what is more knowable by nature. In the Posterior Analytics he explains that what is nearer the senses is more knowable to us. That means starting with particulars and the senses and working towards principles. So we begin with the concrete world: stories, specimens, drawing, music, making things with our hands.

    History is the first story, told as a narrative running from the Big Bang to breakfast. We return to it in Stage Ε as a question of causes: why states rise, why wars start, why ideas take hold.

    Science follows the instruments. Once a student has language, number, logic and code, nature can be measured and not only admired.

    Philosophy comes twice. Its tools (logic and dialectic) arrive early. Philosophy as a subject (questions about being, knowledge and mind) waits until a student has enough of the world to reason about.

    Ethics and politics come last, on purpose. Aristotle says the young are not proper students of political science because they lack experience of life. Elsewhere he notes that the young can become good mathematicians but not practically wise, because practical wisdom (phronēsis) needs experience of particulars. That is why Stage Ϛ is built around real responsibility.

    Every domain appears at every stage at a different depth, as a spiral and not a ladder. The stages use Greek numerals, including ϛ for six. Ages are a guide for a young student; an adult can move through the early stages in months.

    To begin on Monday, follow the starter path: twelve weeks of Stage Α for a child, or a month for an adult.

    VIThe machine

    Rules for the tutor

    An AI tutor is a powerful teaching instrument and an easy one to misuse. These rules keep it an Aristotle rather than a ghost-writer.

    The tutor prompts put these rules into words any AI model will follow. Every stage and every part of the World View has its own; this is the base they share.

    1. Attempt first.The student tries before the machine speaks. A first attempt that fails still does most of the learning.
    2. The tutor asks more than it tells.Its default mode is Socratic. It gives answers only after the student has reasoned.
    3. Never outsource the making.It may critique the essay, the proof or the code. It may not write them.
    4. Verify everything.Machines can be confidently wrong. Trace every claim to a primary source, a measurement or a proof.
    5. Use it as an opponent.Ask it to argue the other side at full strength, then answer it.
    6. Explain it back.If you can’t teach it to the machine, and then to a younger companion, you don’t know it.
    7. Keep the notebook by hand.The machine remembers for you. The notebook makes you remember.

    ΚαθηγητήςFor whoever teaches

    The mentor’s guide

    Aristotle had one pupil of note at Mieza and a few companions beside him. You may have one child, a family or a small cohort. This page is what the mentor needs to run the days, the weeks and the conversations, and what to watch for as a learner grows. It is our reading of the method, not a record of Aristotle’s lessons.

    The jobDay and weekSymposiumField dayThe machineMentor hourStages
    IThe job

    Ask, set the rhythm, keep the rules

    You are not the source of answers. The library and the machine hold more than any mentor. Your work is to choose what comes next, ask the question that opens it and judge whether it has been learnt.

    Guard the rhythm. Hard things early, the body every day, a rest day every week. A good week is mostly the same as the last one, with one new thing in it.

    Model what you ask for. Keep your own notebook. Read in front of them. Say “I don’t know, let’s find out” often, and then find out.

    Be questioned. The rules in Curriculum II apply to you. When a learner catches you out, thank them in front of the others.

    IIDay and week

    Running the day and the week

    The timings are in Start here for Stage Α and in Curriculum V for later stages. These are the habits that make them work.

    Plan the week on Sunday evening, on one sheet: the week’s focus, the reading, the making, the field trip, the symposium question. Pin it where everyone can see it.

    Start each day the same way. Out of doors first, then the hardest work while minds are fresh. No screens in the morning except the tutor, and only with the rules.

    End each day with the notebook. Five minutes for a young child, twenty for an older one. Read it weekly, never mark it, and ask about one entry.

    Keep the rest day. Nothing set, nothing checked. Scholē means leisure, and the habit of real leisure is part of the curriculum.

    Once a month, show. The lunar evening is the deadline that makes things get finished. Once a term, show something to people outside the household.

    IIIThe symposium

    Running the symposium

    One question, one meal, everyone speaks. It trains the habits of Curriculum II: state a view fairly before you attack it, and attack arguments, not people.

    1. Choose the question in advance.Open, concrete and arguable: “Should Achilles have gone back to fight?”, not “What is courage?”. Announce it a day ahead so people come with a view.
    2. Everyone speaks once before anyone speaks twice.Go round the table, youngest first. The mentor speaks last, if at all.
    3. Restate before you rebut.Before disagreeing, say the other view back until its owner agrees you have it right.
    4. Ask for reasons and cases.“Why do you think so?” and “Can you give an example?” do most of the work. Avoid giving your own answer.
    5. End with what is still open.Close by naming the best unanswered question. It goes in everyone’s notebook and may be next week’s question.
    IVField day

    The field day

    Aristotle collected; so do we. The point is to meet the real thing: the shore, the workshop, the court, the building site.

    1. Go somewhere real work or real nature happens.A rock pool beats an aquarium; a working joiner’s shop beats a craft centre. Ask ahead: most people are glad to show their work.
    2. Bring three questions and a notebook.Agree the questions beforehand. Draw at least one thing; drawing makes you look.
    3. Ask one expert one good question.The learner asks, not the mentor. Write down the answer in their words.
    4. Bring something back.A specimen for the cabinet, a photograph, a measurement, a sketch. Then write it up the next day, while it is fresh.
    VThe machine

    Using the AI tutor well

    The rules are in Curriculum VI. Every stage and every part of the World View has a tutor prompt that encodes them. The mentor’s job is to see they are kept.

    Start every session from a prompt. Copy the stage or sub-area prompt rather than typing a bare question. It tells the tutor to ask before it tells and never to do the work.

    Sit with young children. Under ten, an adult holds the device and reads the replies aloud. Sessions are short: one question explored, then back to the world.

    Check the notebook, not the chat. What the learner wrote by hand is what they learnt. If the notebook is thin and the chat is long, something is wrong.

    Test for outsourcing by asking for an explanation. Polished work the learner cannot explain back, line by line, was not theirs. Ask kindly, and start again from the attempt.

    Spot-check a claim a week. Pick one thing the tutor said and trace it to a source together. Sometimes it will be wrong; that lesson is worth the time.

    Keep personal details out. No full names, addresses or school names in prompts. Our prompts never ask for them.

    VIOne to one

    The mentor hour

    One to one, as Aristotle with Alexander. It covers character, friendships, plans, doubts and health. Personal matters count as curriculum.

    Walk if you can. Side by side is easier than face to face. Twenty minutes for a young child, an hour for an adult.

    Use the same four questions. What went well? What puzzled you? What was hard? What next? Then follow wherever the answers lead.

    Listen more than you talk. Silence is allowed. Give advice only when asked, or when something matters too much to wait.

    Keep a short private note after each hour: one line on how they are, one on what you agreed. Read back over a term to see the pattern.

    Keep confidences, except where someone’s safety is at risk. Say so at the start, so it is never a surprise.
